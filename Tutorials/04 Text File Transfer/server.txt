Game theory is a mathematical framework 
for analyzing situations in which the outcomes depend on the 
interactions of multiple decision-makers, often called players. 

    These players can be individuals, groups, firms, or nations, 
and their decisions or strategies impact not only their own outcomes 
but also those of others involved. Game theory seeks to model and predict rational 
behavior in competitive or cooperative environments by focusing on strategies, payoffs, 
and equilibrium points. At its core, game theory assumes that players are rational and will make
 decisions to maximize their utility or benefit. One of the foundational concepts of game theory 
 is the Nash equilibrium, named after mathematician John Nash, which occurs when no player can 
 unilaterally improve their payoff by changing their strategy while the others keep theirs 
 constant. This equilibrium provides insight into stable outcomes where each player's strategy is 
 optimal given the strategies of others. Game theory can be applied to a wide range of scenarios, 
 from simple two-player games like the Prisoner's Dilemma to complex multi-player games involving 
 intricate interactions. The Prisoner’s Dilemma is a classic example where two players must 
 decide whether to cooperate or defect without knowing the other's choice. 
 
 
 
 If both cooperate, 
 they receive a moderate reward; if one defects while the other cooperates, the defector gets a 
 higher reward while the cooperator suffers; and if both defect, they receive lower rewards than 
 if they had cooperated. This paradox highlights the tension between individual rationality and 
 collective benefit, as mutual cooperation leads to the best overall outcome but is difficult to 
 achieve without trust or external enforcement. 
 
                        Another important concept in game theory is 
 zero-sum games, where one player's gain is precisely balanced by the losses of others, such as 
 in chess or poker. In contrast, non-zero-sum games allow for mutually beneficial outcomes, as 
 seen in trade negotiations or cooperative business strategies. Extensive-form games represent 
 situations where players make decisions sequentially, often depicted as decision trees, allowing 
 analysts to account for timing and information availability. On the other hand, normal-form 
 games use matrices to summarize strategies and payoffs when players act simultaneously. The 
 concept of dominance is crucial in game theory, where a dominant strategy is one that yields the 
 best outcome for a player regardless of the choices of others, while dominated strategies are 
 suboptimal and can be eliminated from consideration. Mixed strategies, involving probabilistic 
 choices between different actions, are another key element, especially in games without 
 pure-strategy equilibria, such as Rock-Paper-Scissors. Game theory extends beyond simple 
 two-player scenarios to encompass cooperative games, where binding agreements are possible, and 
 non-cooperative games, where players act independently. Cooperative game theory focuses on 
 coalitions, shared resources, and the fair distribution of payoffs, using concepts like the 
 Shapley value and the Core to measure fairness and stability. In non-cooperative settings, the 
 focus is on individual incentives and strategic moves, often modeled using payoff matrices or 
 utility functions. Game theory has profound implications in economics, political science, 
 biology, computer science, and more. In economics, it explains market dynamics, auction design, pricing strategies, and competition between firms.
